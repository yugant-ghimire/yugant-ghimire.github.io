## Part 1: What the EU AI Act Actually Is

### The Core Idea

The EU AI Act is not a ban on AI. It's not a tax on innovation. At its heart, it's a **risk-based  framework**: the more dangerous your AI system's potential consequences for real people, the more you have to demonstrate, before deploying it, that you've addressed those consequences.

Think of it the same way you think about regulated industries you already know. A website doesn't need safety certification. A medical device does. A blog post doesn't need a pharmaceutical trial. A drug does. The EU AI Act applies that same intuition to AI: a spam filter is not the same as a system that decides whether someone gets a loan.

The framework has four tiers:
- **Prohibited**: AI practices that are banned outright.
- **High-Risk AI**: significant obligations before deployment.
- **Limited Transparency Risk**: AI that interacts with users or generates content; needs disclosure requirements.
- **Minimal Risk**: Everything else. It is basically unregulated, voluntary codes of conduct only.

### What's Already Live — The Developer Timeline

![EU AI Act timeline]({{ page.post_assets | relative_url }}/eu_ai_act_timeline.png)


The Act doesn't hit all at once. Here's where things stand right now:

**February 2, 2025 — The Hard Bans Are Alive**

Eight categories of AI are now illegal in the EU. Not "discouraged." Not "requires review." Illegal, with fines up to €35 million or 7% of global annual turnover for violations. These include:
- AI that manipulates people subliminally or exploits their psychological vulnerabilities
- Government systems that score citizens on their social behavior (social credit systems)
- Real-time facial recognition surveillance in public spaces (with very narrow law enforcement exceptions)
- AI that scrapes the internet or CCTV footage to build facial recognition databases
- Emotion recognition systems in schools and workplaces
- AI that infers race, religion, sexual orientation, or political views from biometric data
- AI that predicts criminal behavior based on profiling or personal characteristics alone

If any product you're working on, that you're planning on deploying to the EU, does any of these things: that's your product design problem right now, today, not in 2027.

**August 2, 2025 — Foundation Model Obligations Are Live**

If you use OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, or any other foundation model provider to build products for EU users, the upstream provider now has binding obligations to you under the Act. They must provide technical documentation, model cards, acceptable use policies, training data summaries, and copyright compliance information. The EU AI Office, a dedicated Commission body, is now operational and has enforcement authority over these providers.

**August 2, 2026 — The Main Deadline**

This is when the bulk of obligations land for high-risk AI systems: conformity assessments, documentation requirements, human oversight mandates, EU AI database registration, transparency disclosures. This is the date everyone is scrambling toward.

**Possible Extension — The Digital Omnibus**

In November 2025, under significant pressure from US tech companies and companies within EU, the European Commission proposed the "Digital Omnibus on AI", a package that would delay full high-risk AI compliance to December 2027 for some categories. The AI Omnibus has already entered the final stages of negotiations between the European Parliament and the Council, with discussions underway as of early 2026. Take this as a sign to align your product with the EU AI Act and draft policies within your organization to document AI systems and analyze/modify parts that fall into the danger category of the Act. 

### The Eight Things You Cannot Build

Let's make the prohibitions concrete. The EU AI Act does not ban AI in general rather it bans specific practices. A facial recognition system in a controlled access system is not banned. A system that scrapes the open web to build a facial recognition database is. The line is drawn at practices that create systemic risk to fundamental rights without meaningful consent or oversight.

The practical impact for founders and developers: **audit your product's core use case before you go further.** If your product infers emotions from facial expressions in a classroom setting, you have a legal problem that no amount of compliance documentation will fix. If your product ranks job candidates based on their social media activity correlated with protected characteristics, that's the same problem. The time to pivot is now — not after you've raised your Series A on a product that's banned in your biggest market.

### What "High-Risk AI" Means in Practice

The Act designates eight categories of high-risk AI. Written out plainly, for developers:

1. **Biometrics** — remote facial recognition, emotion recognition, biometric categorization
2. **Critical infrastructure** — AI managing power grids, water systems, transport networks
3. **Education** — automated grading, admissions algorithms, learning outcome prediction
4. **Employment** — CV screening, candidate ranking, performance monitoring, promotion/termination systems
5. **Essential services** — credit scoring, insurance underwriting, healthcare triage, emergency dispatch
6. **Law enforcement** — crime risk prediction, recidivism assessment, criminal profiling
7. **Migration** — asylum decision support, border control risk scoring
8. **Justice and democracy** — judicial decision assistance, electoral influence systems

Here's the key message for founders: **if you're building for HR, fintech, edtech, healthtech, or any application touching access to essential services, you are building high-risk AI.** The compliance burden is not optional and does not scale down because you're a startup. Plan ahead, conduct regular literacy programs within your organization for the correct practices and document your system as per the requirement of the Act.
