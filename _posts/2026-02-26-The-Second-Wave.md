### Introduction: The Second Wave

Cast your mind back to May 2018. The General Data Protection Regulation went into full effect across Europe. Suddenly, every tech company on earth, regardless of where their servers were, had a new set of rules to follow. Startups rewrote their privacy policies. Engineers retrofitted data minimization into systems built on the assumption that more data was always better. "GDPR compliance" became a line item in product roadmaps all over the world. Even California passed the Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA) just one month after GDPR's enforcement date, in June 2018.

The market influence of Europe is immense. The EU has led the world in regulating technology that directly affects end users. The regulations were European. The impact was global. That's the Brussels Effect in action: when a market the size of the EU sets a regulatory standard, the world tends to follow; not because it has to, legally, but because companies serving that market cannot afford to maintain separate compliance systems for separate geographies.

We are standing at the beginning of the second wave.

The EU Artificial Intelligence Act: Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, 144 pages of binding law, entered into force on August 1, 2024. Its main enforcement date is **August 2, 2026**. That is less than four months away at the time of writing. According to a late 2025 survey cited by JAIKIN, fewer than 30% of European SMEs have begun steps toward EU AI Act compliance. The majority still believe the regulation only affects large technology companies, which is a significant misconception. The rest of the world is even further behind.

When GDPR was introduced, it forced a fundamental shift in software architecture, requiring developers to bake "Privacy by Design" directly into their code. Building a standard application that stored user information suddenly meant engineering automated pipelines for Data Subject Access Requests (DSARs), ensuring that users could seamlessly export or completely erase their data, along with restructuring database schemas to enforce strict data minimization. Now, the EU AI Act is poised to introduce a similarly rigorous paradigm shift. If GDPR forced developers to think like privacy experts, the EU AI Act will require anyone building AI systems to think like safety engineers. The obligations are highly specific and technical; if you are developing anything classified as high-risk, particularly systems touching hiring, credit, healthcare, education, or public services, the burden of implementing robust logging, bias testing, and human-oversight mechanisms will be substantial.

This article is the first in a five-part series on the EU AI Act and its implications for the world, concluding with a close look at Nepal. It addresses two questions. First, what does the EU AI Act actually require, explained in terms developers and founders can act on, not just lawyers can bill for? Second, what does this mean for Nepal specifically, a country at a genuine inflection point in its technology ambitions, and why is Nepal's current AI governance framework, while directionally right, still missing the structural foundations that would allow it to participate meaningfully in the post-EU-AI-Act global economy?
